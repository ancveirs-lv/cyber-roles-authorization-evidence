# Incident commentary risk

Public communication can help society, customers, and defenders. It can also become part of incident risk when unverified information is published too early or in excessive detail.

## Potential harm

| Action | Risk |
|---|---|
| Naming a cause or culprit prematurely | Misattribution, reputational harm, investigative distraction |
| Revealing defender observations and reactions | The attacker learns what has been detected and what to change |
| Publishing vulnerable assets or unremediated technical detail | Reuse against the same or similar systems |
| Repeating attacker claims without labelling them | Amplification of disinformation and extortion pressure |
| Publishing personal or sensitive data | Additional harm to victims and legal exposure |
| Making absolute conclusions with external-commentator access | False public confidence in the quality of evidence |

The [NCSC incident-communications guidance](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/guidance/effective-communications-in-a-cyber-incident) advises against speculation and premature conclusions, and against prejudicing regulatory or law-enforcement investigations.

## Safe minimum for a public update

1. What is confirmed now?
2. What remains unknown?
3. What practical next step should affected people take?
4. When and where will the next official update appear?
5. Which details are being withheld for now, and why?

## The “Red Cap Team” boundary

In this project, *Red Cap Team* is an explicitly labelled editorial metaphor for a commentator who borrows a practitioner's visual or rhetorical authority. It is not an industry term and should not be used to defame a particular person.

The more precise approach is to criticise a specific behaviour: missing sources, presenting hypotheses as facts, disclosing sensitive detail, or failing to correct an error.
